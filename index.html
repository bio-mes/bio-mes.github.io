Biological multi-expert system

BioMes

Biological hypotheses, generated by simulated expert discussion.

BioMes is a biological multi-expert system that frames hypothesis generation as a dynamic discussion among LLM-simulated experts with complementary disciplinary perspectives — in biochemistry, biophysics, cell biology, and more — who contribute evidence, expose hidden assumptions, and are synthesized and critiqued into mechanistically grounded, testable hypotheses. On FutureBio, a temporally held-out benchmark, BioMes substantially outperforms general-purpose LLMs and representative agent systems — and in a prospective autophagy study, human-in-the-loop experiments guided by BioMes identified NMT2 as a previously unrecognized regulator of autophagy.

The problem

The bottleneck isn't more data. It's connecting it across disciplines.

Many of biology's most consequential advances stemmed from hypotheses that synthesized disparate evidence into testable mechanistic explanations. As life-science knowledge expands in technical depth and disciplinary breadth, this is getting harder. The challenge is not simply the growing scale of data, but integrating heterogeneous, incomplete evidence scattered across papers, databases, and experimental contexts from different disciplines. LLM-based agents such as Co-Scientist, Robin, and the AI Scientist have recast hypothesis generation as an executable task, decomposing it into literature retrieval, evidence synthesis, reasoning, and proposal. But biological hypothesis generation should not be reduced to a sequence of computational modules: in practice, hypotheses are sharpened through discussion among researchers from different disciplines, who contribute complementary knowledge, expose hidden assumptions, identify contradictions, and weigh claims against mechanistic and experimental constraints. Multi-agent systems should move toward simulated expert communities.

How BioMes works

From question to critiqued hypothesis — and back again.

01

Assemble

Instantiate relevant domain-specific experts for the biological question.

02

Discuss

Experts search evidence, propose mechanisms, challenge assumptions, and identify contradictions.

03

Synthesize

Distill consensus, disagreement, evidence, and candidate mechanisms.

04

Critique

Evaluate hypotheses for evidential support, logical consistency, novelty, and experimental actionability.

Steps 02–04 aren't a straight line: the critic can raise new sub-questions that send the discussion back for another round, until the hypothesis is fully grounded.

Results

Two tests: a benchmark, and a real discovery.

BioMes is first evaluated on FutureBio, a benchmark that tests whether it generates new hypotheses rather than memorizing existing knowledge. It is then tested prospectively, using autophagy as a wet-lab case study.

Computational evaluation

Can AI infer tomorrow's biology from yesterday's evidence?

FutureBio uses central findings from temporally held-out life-science papers — published after the evaluated models' release dates — as verifiable targets for hypothesis evaluation. Combining temporal separation with restricted literature access, it objectively tests whether LLM agents can infer new biological hypotheses from prior evidence, rather than recall existing knowledge or produce plausible but hard-to-validate conjectures.

The gain isn't just a stronger backbone: on both GPT-5-Mini and GPT-5.5, BioMes outperforms tool-augmented, ReAct, and orchestrated-workflow agents built on the same underlying model, supporting simulated expert deliberation as an effective strategy.

Prospective wet-lab validation

From an open question to a confirmed regulator.

Autophagy is a conserved, lysosome-dependent degradation pathway that recycles cytoplasmic components, damaged proteins, and organelles; its dysregulation is implicated in neurodegeneration, cancer, and metabolic disease. Mechanistic advances in the field span cell and molecular biology, biochemistry, biophysics, genetics, and pathology, while proteomic and spatial datasets now catalogue thousands of proteins and their subcellular distributions — a candidate space that exceeds manual literature analysis. We asked whether BioMes could turn this knowledge into experimentally actionable hypotheses: which Golgi-localized proteins are most likely to regulate autophagy, and through what mechanisms?

Golgi proteins → BioMes discussion → Top 20 candidates → Wet-lab validation → NMT2
Experimental outcome

NMT2

Through BioMes-guided, human-in-the-loop experiments, we identified NMT2, one of two mammalian N-myristoyltransferases, as a previously unrecognized regulator of autophagy. Mechanistically, NMT2 promotes autophagosome biogenesis by maintaining Golgi organization and facilitating starvation-induced membrane contacts between the ER-Golgi intermediate compartment (ERGIC) and ER exit sites (ERES).

Looking ahead

An expert-deliberation paradigm for LLM agents, validated by benchmark and experiment: together, these results point toward human-in-the-loop agent systems that substantially accelerate experimentally grounded discovery in biology.
